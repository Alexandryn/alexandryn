CHAPTER V · SECOND DAY — TERCE

The scriptorium was already cold when we entered it, and the light that came through the alabaster windows had the colour of watered milk. Thirty desks stood in two rows, each with its own share of the morning.

My master walked the length of the room without speaking. He had a way of looking at a place that made the place seem to have been arranged for his inspection, and the monks, who had at first pretended not to notice us, began one by one to lift their heads.

“Every library is a plan of the mind that built it,” he said at last, quietly, so that only I could hear. “Learn the plan and you will know what the builder was afraid of.”

I asked him what he meant, and he did not answer, because the librarian had come toward us with his hands folded and his face composed into the particular blankness that men use when they have decided in advance what they will refuse.

Outside, the bell rang for terce. The snow had begun again, and the whole valley below the abbey was going white, one field at a time, in the unhurried way of things that do not care whether they are watched.

The scriptorium was already cold when we entered it, and the light that came through the alabaster windows had the colour of watered milk. Thirty desks stood in two rows, each with its own share of the morning, and at each desk a man bent over work he would not explain to me.

I asked him what he meant, and he did not answer, because the librarian had come toward us with his hands folded and his face composed into the particular blankness that men use when they have decided in advance what they will refuse.

Outside, the bell rang for terce. The snow had begun again, and the whole valley below the abbey was going white, one field at a time, in the unhurried way of things that do not care whether they are watched.
